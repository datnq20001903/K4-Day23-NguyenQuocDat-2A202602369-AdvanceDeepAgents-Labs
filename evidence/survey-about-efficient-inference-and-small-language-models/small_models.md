Synthesis: Compact performance comes from complementary strategies: richer teacher supervision (rationales or synthetic instruction-response corpora) can improve data efficiency for task-focused students, while sparse MoE designs keep active compute low by activating only a subset of parameters. These are different trade-offs: distillation concentrates a teacher's behavior into a smaller dense student, whereas MoE retains larger total capacity and therefore still requires substantial memory; instruction and preference tuning shape usability but do not by themselves guarantee general capability.

Evidence gaps: the sources use non-uniform benchmarks and settings, so there is no clean cross-paper ranking or single recipe that separates data, architecture, and post-training effects. Further matched-compute evaluations are needed for latency, memory, energy, contamination/robustness, and broad capabilities; teacher-generated data also warrants explicit checks for inherited errors and biases. Pythia contributes controlled study of training dynamics, but is not itself evidence for a universally superior small-model recipe.
