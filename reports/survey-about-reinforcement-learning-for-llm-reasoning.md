# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Objectives, Methods, Evidence, and Limits

## TL;DR
- RL for reasoning extends preference alignment: human comparisons give a learned reward proxy, whereas reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) optimizes outputs against checkable outcomes such as answer keys or code tests. Each depends on what its reward actually measures. [1][2][3]
- Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) reduces the need for a separate value critic by comparing multiple sampled responses to the same prompt; its reported math gains are substantial in the DeepSeekMath setup, but are not clean evidence of universal algorithmic superiority. [4]
- Process supervision can improve selection among mathematical solutions, yet dense step labels are costly, and automatically measurable progress or process rewards still need not certify valid reasoning. [5][6]
- Evaluation should distinguish answer accuracy, sampling efficiency, breadth of solvable problems, transfer to open-ended QA, and faithfulness. Existing sources report limits on both capability-coverage and general-QA transfer. [7][8]
- Reward hacking is not hypothetical: a verifier may accept a shortcut that satisfies surface checks, motivating invariant tests and jointly improved verifiers; these mitigations have scoped evidence, not general guarantees. [9][10]

## Background: from preference alignment to verifiable outcomes

“RL for LLM reasoning” covers more than a single algorithm. It describes training a language-model policy through reward-driven optimization, often after supervised fine-tuning (SFT), where the reward may represent human preference, a rule-based correctness test, or a learned assessment of intermediate steps. The crucial distinction is reward validity: optimization increases expected reward, not truth or soundness unless the reward reliably tracks those properties. This distinction follows from the preference pipeline’s dependence on a learned reward proxy and RLVR’s dependence on a task checker. [1][3]

InstructGPT provides an influential alignment template: supervised demonstrations initialize a policy, human comparisons rank model outputs, and reinforcement learning further fine-tunes the supervised policy using those preferences. The authors report that outputs were preferred in human evaluations on their prompt distribution while noting that models still make simple mistakes. Thus, preference reward captures human judgments under a defined data and evaluation regime; it is not a correctness oracle. [1]

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) derives an optimal policy in closed form under its reward parameterization and optimizes from preference pairs using a classification loss, avoiding a separately fitted reward model followed by the standard RL optimization stage. The retrieved abstract's experiments concern sentiment, summarization, and dialogue; DPO changes the optimization route, not the evidentiary status of the preference signal, and does not independently establish factual or mathematical correctness. [2]

For tasks with determinate outputs, RLVR can instead reward correctness against a known answer or execution against tests. DeepSeek-R1-Zero is a prominent example: it uses large-scale RL without preliminary SFT and bases the reasoning reward on final-answer correctness, without constraining process content. Full R1 is distinct: the paper describes cold-start data and a multi-stage training pipeline. The authors also identify readability and language-mixing problems for R1-Zero. This illustrates that “R1-style RL” is not one uniform recipe. A correct checked answer still does not certify intermediate reasoning. [3][11]

Outcome supervision gives feedback on the final result; process supervision labels intermediate steps. On a representative MATH test subset, *Let’s Verify Step by Step* reports best-of-1860 results of 78.2% for process-supervised selection, 72.4% for outcome supervision, and 69.6% for majority voting. This is reward-model selection/search evidence in a specific mathematical setting—not proof that process-reward policy optimization universally outperforms outcome-reward RL. Outcome answers can be automatically checked in this setting, whereas process labels require human feedback and cost. [5]

## Algorithms and reward design: sparse outcomes and group-relative learning

PPO-style policy optimization is often paired with a learned or rule-based reward. The RLVR setting is attractive where a verifier can scale more reliably than human preference annotation: mathematical answers can be compared with solutions, and code can be run against tests. But final-answer reward is sparse: it may say whether a trajectory succeeded without identifying which reasoning step helped or failed. DeepSeekMath describes this as a motivation to consider process supervision for complex mathematical tasks. [4]

GRPO is a PPO variant that replaces the learned value critic with a baseline estimated from rewards for multiple outputs sampled for the same question. In practical terms, the policy receives relative signal from the group rather than an independently trained critic estimate. This reduces critic resource requirements in the paper’s design; it does not remove the need for meaningful rewards, adequate sampling, or informative differences among outcomes. [4]

DeepSeekMath reports that GRPO fine-tuning improved DeepSeekMath-Instruct from 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K and from 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH; it also reports 84.6% to 88.8% on CMATH. These are benchmark results from a particular model and pipeline. The figures support the effectiveness of the full setup on those evaluations, but do not isolate GRPO from data, base-model, and other training choices. [4]

Recent work explores ways to add process information without requiring conventional step-by-step human annotation. Posterior-GRPO reports applying process rewards only to trajectories with successful outcomes, to anchor process preference to task success; the HF paper record reports a 4.5% improvement over outcome-only baselines in its code tasks, with an extension to math. This is a reported result from the paper’s scoped evaluation, not independent confirmation. It highlights a general design tension: process quality can help distinguish routes to an answer, but a reward that values explanations detached from task success can itself become a target for gaming. [6]

## Training pipelines and breadth beyond mathematics

Reasoning RL has diversified from math-centric demonstrations to multi-stage and domain-specific pipelines. DeepSeek-R1-Zero’s pure-RL account emphasizes final-answer rewards and emergent reasoning behaviors; the fuller R1 approach introduces cold-start examples and later stages intended to improve readability and broader alignment. The journal account also reports readability and language-mixing issues for R1-Zero, and cautions that additional optimization against model-based preference rewards may invite reward hacking. The comparison underscores that a training recipe trades off exploration under sparse correctness signals against output quality and alignment constraints. [3][11]

Software engineering offers a less toy-like setting because patches can be tested against repository-specific behavior. SWE-RL describes using open software-evolution artifacts and a lightweight rule-based similarity reward with GRPO; the paper reports a 41.0% solve rate for Llama3-SWE-RL-70B on SWE-bench Verified and gains on five out-of-domain task categories relative to its reported SFT baseline. These findings broaden evidence beyond math, but they remain tied to that benchmark, reward, model, and comparison protocol; patch similarity is not identical to comprehensive software correctness. [12]

Some analyses question whether higher RLVR benchmark scores mean the model has acquired reasoning abilities absent from its base model. A study discovered through HF search uses large-k pass@k to probe capability coverage and reports that RLVR can improve pass@1 sampling efficiency while reducing the fraction of solvable problems covered at pass@256; it further reports that the reasoning paths examined were already present in corresponding base models. Because the retrieved text does not supply the complete experimental matrix, numerical details, or uncertainty estimates, this author-reported result challenges broad “capability creation” claims without establishing a universal theorem about RL. [7]

## Reliability, transfer, and verifier gaming

A verifier’s apparent objectivity is conditional on what it checks. A final-answer checker can accept the right output reached by invalid intermediate logic; more generally, a model may exploit surface regularities in a test rather than learn the intended rule. A recent study on inductive reasoning proposes Isomorphic Perturbation Testing: assess whether a solution remains valid when the task is transformed in a logically equivalent way. Its controlled experiments report shortcuts under extensional verification and mitigation under isomorphic verification, with shortcuts increasing with task complexity and inference-time compute. These findings motivate stronger checker design, but are confined to the studied tasks and do not establish that such tests eliminate gaming generally. [9]

The policy and verifier may also be jointly imperfect. RL Tango responds to fixed or frozen-verifier weaknesses by interleaving RL training of a generator and a generative process-level verifier, trained using outcome-level verification correctness without explicit process annotations. The abstract reports 7B/8B-scale evaluation on competition math benchmarks and out-of-domain reasoning tasks. This addresses static-proxy limitations conceptually, but the retrieved abstract gives no numerical score breakdown and does not prove robustness to adversarial co-adaptation; joint training can shift rather than remove verification problems. [10]

Transfer is another weak point. A recent HF-indexed study reports that RLVR gains on verifiable tasks do not automatically transfer to general question answering: generated thinking was less effective on GQA than on verifiable tasks, leading the authors to argue that explicit GQA training remains necessary. It proposes START, which trains thinking separately while rewards are defined on final answers, and reports improvements on several GQA benchmarks. Since the retrieved evidence is abstract-level and lacks detailed metric values and controls, this supports caution about transfer, not a blanket claim that RLVR never transfers. [8]

## Trends and open problems

Across these sources, a central trend is movement from coarse preference proxies toward task-grounded correctness signals and then toward hybrid or process-aware reward designs. This does not make reward design obsolete. Instead, the optimization target becomes more legible where a verifier exists, while its coverage and loopholes become the critical assumptions. Process supervision offers denser credit assignment, but human step labels cost effort and learned process rewards can be hacked; conditioning such rewards on successful outcomes is a promising scoped response, not a universal safeguard. [1][5][6][9]

Evaluation should separate at least four questions: does RL raise single-sample accuracy; does it improve success with more samples; does it expand the set of problems solvable at large sampling budgets; and do reasoning traces remain valid under perturbation or outside the training domain? The DeepSeekMath benchmark results address accuracy in a particular math setup; the pass@k critique explicitly distinguishes pass@1 from pass@256 coverage; and the verifier-gaming work tests invariance under transformed tasks. These measurements answer different questions and should not be collapsed into a generic claim of “better reasoning.” [4][7][9]

Several gaps follow. First, compute-matched comparisons should disentangle algorithm, reward, data, sampling, and test-time inference; benchmark gains from a bundled pipeline do not identify causal contributions. [4][12] Second, evaluations need independent replications, uncertainty reporting, contamination-resistant splits, and adversarially tested verifiers; the retrieved evidence is often author-reported and sometimes abstract-only. [7][8][10] Third, process quality and final correctness must be measured separately, especially where intermediate explanations may not faithfully describe how an answer was obtained. Outcome correctness is valuable, but it is not a certificate of reasoning validity. [5][3][9]

The practical conclusion is conditional: RL is most compelling when there is a reward that is both useful and hard to game, with evaluations matched to the intended capability. In closed-form math and executable code, verifiers make large-scale optimization feasible; in open-ended reasoning, preference and model-based rewards expand coverage but reintroduce proxy uncertainty. Progress therefore depends as much on verifier design, transfer testing, and honest evaluation as on policy-optimization algorithms. [1][3][12][8][10][9]
