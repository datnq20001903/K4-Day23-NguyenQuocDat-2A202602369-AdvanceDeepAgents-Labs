# Video and Multimodal Generation: Architectures, Long-Horizon Control, Evaluation, and Governance

## TL;DR
- Video diffusion developed from image-diffusion extensions into latent-space and large transformer systems, but longer outputs and larger models are not, by themselves, evidence of sustained coherence or predictive physical understanding.[1][2][3][4]
- Multimodal generation spans shared autoregressive sequencing over text and visual embeddings and modular systems that route through specialist encoders and decoders; broad input/output coverage does not establish equivalent native generation quality for every modality.[5][6]
- Long-video research tackles different problems—continuation drift, global control, prompt-boundary transitions, and audio-conditioned facial motion—and its duration demonstrations, benchmarks, and metrics are not directly comparable.[7][8][9][10]
- Multidimensional benchmarks complement targeted evaluations: aggregate visual scores can miss disappearing objects, pose failures, transition discontinuities, or harmful sequences.[11][12][13]
- Dataset provenance audits and system cards illuminate different parts of governance, but neither proves the lineage or safety effectiveness of a particular model; independent corpus audits and robust temporal tests remain open needs.[14][15]

## Background: from video diffusion to multimodal generation
Video generation synthesizes temporally ordered visual output, often conditioned on text, images, or other signals. Multimodal generation is broader: a system may understand, condition on, or generate across text, image, video, and audio. Those capabilities should not be conflated: accepting video as context does not itself mean a model generates temporally dense video.[5][6]

A foundational approach extends image diffusion to video. Video Diffusion Models jointly train on image and video data and introduce conditional sampling for spatial and temporal extension.[1] Their abstract says joint training reduces minibatch-gradient variance and speeds optimization, but the available abstract excerpt does not verify every architectural detail sometimes attributed to its video U-Net.[1] Latent Video Diffusion instead performs diffusion and denoising in a low-dimensional 3D latent space; the authors report autoregressive generation of thousands of frames despite training on tens of frames and introduce conditional latent perturbation to reduce long-duration degradation.[2] That reported capacity is not proof of uniform coherence at every duration.[2]

Recent text-to-video systems use compressed video representations and large denoisers. Step-Video-T2V's abstract describes a 30-billion-parameter model, a video VAE with 16-by-16 spatial and 8-by temporal compression, a 3D full-attention diffusion transformer, and flow matching; it reports videos up to 204 frames.[3] The same report proposes a distinction between cross-modal “translation” and future-oriented “prediction,” placing current diffusion T2V systems in the former category.[3] This is the authors' taxonomy, not a demonstrated test that generation lacks all learned dynamics.[3] Open-Sora 2.0 reports comparisons to leading systems based on human evaluation and VBench, with 100 prompts spanning visual quality, prompt adherence, and motion quality.[4] Those results are author-reported and protocol-specific; the retrieved verification does not support asserting the draft's detailed architectural ingredients or exact scores.[4]

Multimodal systems differ in how they unify modalities. Emu represents visual signals as embeddings interleaved with text tokens and describes a next-element objective that predicts text tokens or visual embeddings.[5] Its abstract describes image-and-text generation and video question answering, but does not establish native temporally dense video synthesis.[5] NExT-GPT connects an LLM through multimodal adaptors to specialist encoders and diffusion decoders, and describes input/output combinations involving text, images, video, and audio.[6] Its claim that about 1% of certain projection parameters are tuned is limited to those parameters, not the entire system or every training phase.[6] These designs trade shared sequencing against modular routing; neither retrieved source establishes uniform high-quality synchronized audio-video generation across arbitrary tasks.[5][6]

## Theme 1: long horizons, control, and multimodal conditioning
Long outputs pose problems beyond frame quality: appearance can drift across chunks, motion can stagnate, controls can lose influence, and successive prompts can produce discontinuities. Methods target distinct parts of that problem. StreamingT2V uses short-term conditional attention, an appearance-preservation module anchored to the first chunk, and randomized blending.[7] It showcases outputs up to 1,200 frames (two minutes), but its quantitative tests use 50 prompts and 240-frame generations.[7] The paper reports motion-aware warp error (MAWE) of 52.3 and scene-cut count (SCuts) of 0.04 in its table, and characterizes MAWE as nearly 30% lower than the next-best comparator.[7] The distinction between demonstration and scored length is essential: those metrics do not validate the two-minute examples.[7] The authors also warn that near-static output can score well on SCuts, so the metric is not an adequate stand-alone measure of dynamic quality.[7]

LongVie emphasizes long-range control and degradation, combining autoregressive generation, unified noise initialization, globally normalized control signals, dense depth and sparse keypoint control, and degradation-aware training.[8] Its LongVGenBench contains 100 high-resolution videos longer than one minute; the reported evaluation segments references into clips, including ten 49-frame clips at 8 fps with one-frame overlap.[8] The paper's abstract claims state-of-the-art results, but its reported table varies by dimension: its scores include 87.12% subject consistency, 91.76% background consistency, 46.59% dynamic degree, SSIM 0.557, and LPIPS 0.290, and it is not best on every listed measure.[8] These are paper-reported results on a finite benchmark and segmented protocol, not a demonstration of indefinite generation or a direct comparison with other studies' scores.[8]

DiTCtrl treats multi-prompt generation as temporal editing, using attention sharing and mask-guided semantic control without additional training.[9] Its MPVBench includes 130 prompts across ten transition types; the automatic table reports CSCV 84.90%, motion smoothness 97.80%, and text-image similarity 30.68%, while FreeNoise has higher text-image similarity at 32.69%.[9] A 28-participant study reports an overall preference score of 4.11 on a 1-to-5 scale.[9] CSCV is a proposed proxy intended to expose localized discontinuities that an average CLIP-style score can dilute, not a universally validated perceptual measure.[9] Thus the methods are complementary rather than rankable: StreamingT2V evaluates chunk extension, LongVie evaluates long-video control on reference segments, and DiTCtrl evaluates transitions between prompts.[7][8][9]

Audio-conditioned generation adds another modality-specific control setting. SkyReels-Audio describes talking-portrait generation and editing conditioned on audio alongside text, image, or video inputs, and claims “infinite-length” capability.[10] Its evaluation excerpt describes 100 test clips and task-specific synchronization and image-quality measures; one HDTF comparison reports Sync-C 6.06 and FVD 364.71, while its lip-sync comparison lists Sync-C 8.49 versus 8.48 for LatenSync and slightly lower IQA (3.62 versus 3.63).[10] Such results support task-specific audio/face alignment experiments, not unlimited-duration performance: the retrieved evidence includes no scored infinite-length test or operational maximum.[10]

## Theme 2: evaluation and failure modes
Video quality is multidimensional. VBench++ organizes evaluation into 16 dimensions using tailored prompts, specialized automatic methods, and human preference annotations; its dimensions include identity consistency, motion smoothness, flicker, and spatial relationships.[11] It also describes trustworthiness dimensions such as cultural fairness, human bias, and safety.[11] VBench-2.0 instead emphasizes intrinsic faithfulness—human fidelity, controllability, creativity, physics, and commonsense—and describes combining generalist vision-language/language models, specialist anomaly detectors, and human annotations.[12] These suites expand what is measured, but their scores depend on chosen prompts, evaluators, and dimensions; the evidence does not establish a universal scalar measure of video quality.[11][12]

Targeted tests explain what broad metrics may hide. WYD focuses on visually conditioned human video generation and contains 1,544 captioned clips labeled across nine aspects.[13] Its reported failure modes include identity collapse, disappearing interaction objects, atypical motion, and occlusion; the paper notes that FVD may barely change when a disappearing object occupies only a small part of a frame.[13] Pose metrics can expose motion-adherence errors, but the paper also reports that pose estimators can be incorrect or temporally incoherent.[13] Accordingly, benchmark findings are informative for this human-centric image-to-video task, not a complete measure of all video or audio-video generation.[13]

Safety likewise has a temporal dimension. T2VSafetyBench's proceedings version defines four categories and 14 aspects, including cases where individually benign frames form harmful sequences through continuity or change.[14] The paper reports that its evaluation samples one frame per second and uses GPT-4 assessments alongside manual review; due to compute costs and model blocking, the experiments use a 689-prompt subset and assess nine T2V models. These protocol details are reported in the paper-specific evidence notes, though the audit retrieval verified the proceedings abstract's category/aspect count and model count more directly than the sampling and subset passages.[14] The paper reports that no tested model excels across all aspects.[14] Sparse frame sampling, a curated prompt set, and model-based judging limit what this establishes about transient events or real-world deployment.[14] Inference: since these suites test different constructs and protocols, their raw results cannot support a shared model ranking without harmonized prompts, generation settings, sampling, and judges.[11][13][14]

## Theme 3: data provenance, safety, and deployment governance
An ecosystem audit offers evidence on dataset documentation rather than on any specific generator. It manually examines nearly 4,000 public datasets.[15] The paper-specific evidence notes describe a video subset of 104 datasets across 33 tasks and report that 36% lack license or intended-use documentation; they also note source restrictions can be obscured through repackaging.[15] The same notes report that over 80% of source content in widely used text, speech, and video datasets carries non-commercial restrictions, although fewer than one-third of datasets are restrictively licensed.[15] These detailed figures are supported by the researcher notes but were not directly surfaced in the audit's returned excerpt; they concern the audited public-dataset ecosystem, not the complete training corpus of a private model, and do not determine legal liability.[15]

Provider system cards describe stated mitigations but are not independent audits. OpenAI's Sora 2 card, dated September 30, 2025, identifies nonconsensual likeness use and misleading generations as risks and says training data include publicly available internet information and third-party partnerships, as well as user-, trainer-, and researcher-supplied or generated data.[16] It describes safety models scanning video frames, audio transcripts, prompts, and scene descriptions, and acknowledges that some deceptive content is highly contextual and not readily detected by classifiers.[16] This is provider-reported disclosure, not a complete itemized corpus inventory or independent evidence that mitigations work in all settings.[16] Inference: dataset documentation, input/output filtering, and temporal safety benchmarks intervene at different stages of the risk lifecycle, and none substitutes for the others.[14][15][16]

## Trends and open problems
The evidence traces a shift from direct diffusion on videos toward compressed latent representations and transformer-based systems, while multimodal models continue to make different choices about shared sequencing versus specialist-module orchestration.[1][2][3][5][6] Long-horizon methods increasingly use memory, anchoring, global controls, or transition-aware attention, but reported durations need to be read alongside scored lengths and test protocols.[7][8][9] Audio conditioning broadens control for talking portraits, yet abstract-level “infinite-length” wording is not validated by finite-duration evaluations.[10]

Evaluation validity remains a central problem. Automatic metrics can reward near-static video, miss small disappearing objects, or depend on fallible pose estimates; proposed transition metrics and broad evaluator-model suites also need validation against human judgments and task outcomes.[7][9][11][12][13] No cited evidence supplies one common, reproducible protocol that jointly measures long-horizon identity, motion, semantic control, physical plausibility, audio-video alignment, and temporal safety. Inference: future comparisons should disclose prompts, duration, sampling, annotator procedures, uncertainty, and detector limitations, given the mismatches and failure cases documented above.[7][9][11][13][14]

Data governance is similarly incomplete. Public dataset audits show documentation gaps, while provider cards offer partial descriptions of data and mitigation processes; neither establishes independent lineage for a specific private model.[15][16] Priority research needs include independently auditable training-data inventories, diverse temporal and multilingual safety evaluations, long-duration control stress tests, and evaluation that distinguishes plausible appearance from coherent motion. These are priorities inferred from the stated scopes and limitations of existing sources, not claims that a particular system has solved them.[8][11][13][14][15]
